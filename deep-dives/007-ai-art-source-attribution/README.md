# Deep dive 007 — AI art, source attribution, and aesthetic judgment

## Purpose

Establish a careful account of the difference between an artwork’s actual production source, a viewer’s belief about that source, and the viewer’s aesthetic response.

## Bounded question

What does Grassini and Koivisto’s 2024 experiment show—and not show—about whether perceived AI authorship changes how viewers evaluate an artwork?

## Why reuse this

The distinction can support criticism of AI art, disclosure practices, provenance systems, authenticity claims, and arguments about authorship without treating visual appearance as conclusive evidence of origin.

## Core reference

- SRC-032 — [Grassini and Koivisto, “Understanding how personality traits, experiences, and attitudes shape negative bias toward AI-generated artworks”](../../sources/academic/SRC-032-grassini-koivisto-ai-art-bias/README.md): candidate primary empirical study of source judgment and image liking.

## Working sessions

### Session 1 — Separate the variables

1. Close-read the study’s materials, procedure, and results.
2. Record separately: true source, participants’ source belief, liking, positive emotion, and the image-selection process.
3. State the experiment’s causal and non-causal claims in plain language.

### Session 2 — Test the interpretation

1. Identify what the selected stimulus set permits us to infer and what it cannot generalize.
2. Compare the study’s attribution effect with accounts of authenticity, authorship, and disclosure.
3. Add a direct counterexample or replication before making a broad claim about “people” or “AI art.”

## Evidence placeholders

### AAB-001 — Actual source and believed source are different variables

- **Prospective finding:** An image can be AI-generated yet believed human-made, or human-made yet believed AI-generated; an analysis must not collapse those conditions.
- **Support:** SRC-032, Procedure and Results.
- **Challenge:** The study uses a continuous source-belief scale and a particular image set, not every real-world disclosure condition.
- **Scope:** The distinction is conceptual and methodological; it does not demonstrate the provenance of any individual image outside the experiment.
- **Status:** needs-close-reading

### AAB-002 — The observed negative association concerns attribution, not intrinsic quality

- **Prospective finding:** In the study, greater belief that an image was AI-generated predicted lower liking and less positive emotion, even though the AI set received higher ratings by actual source.
- **Support:** SRC-032, Results and Discussion.
- **Challenge:** The study is observational with respect to participants’ own source beliefs; it does not randomly assign an identical image a human or AI label.
- **Scope:** Treat this as evidence of an attribution-linked pattern in this sample, not a universal judgment about artistic quality.
- **Status:** needs-close-reading

## Done enough when

- The final account distinguishes actual source, perceived source, disclosure, and aesthetic evaluation.
- The study’s exact stimulus selection, sample, measures, and reported results have locator-level records.
- At least one directly relevant replication, experimental label manipulation, or counterexample qualifies the interpretation.
- Reusable conclusions are placed in `synthesis.md` without turning a candidate source into writer-approved evidence.
