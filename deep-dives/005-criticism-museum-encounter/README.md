# Deep dive 005 — Criticism through museum encounter

## Purpose

Develop a reusable field method in which direct encounters with works test theory. The museum is not a storehouse of illustrations for a prewritten thesis; it is a place where material, scale, sequence, display, labels, and attention can resist abstraction.

## Bounded question

How can situated observation generate better questions about representation, realism, authorship, mediation, and ontology without confusing personal experience with historical evidence?

## Why reuse this

The protocol can support exhibition reviews, field essays, object studies, and any article that needs criticism grounded in an actual encounter.

## Before the visit — 30 minutes

1. Check what is actually on view and current hours.
2. Read your article thesis once, then close it.
3. Choose three questions, not three objects:
   - What counts as representation here?
   - Where is the work's making visible or concealed?
   - What does the display ask me to believe?
4. Prepare voice notes or a notebook. Record gallery and object identifiers before each reflection.
5. Leave room for one object that has no obvious connection to the article.

## Suggested route by problem

Adapt this to current closures and displays. Do not treat it as a chronology of artistic progress.

1. **Ancient Egyptian or ancient Mediterranean:** preservation, ritual presence, likeness, and the relation between image and absent body.
2. **European or Asian galleries:** workshop, attribution, copying, icon, convention, and perspectival systems.
3. **Decorative arts and design:** function, repeated production, craft labor, and the unstable border between art object and tool.
4. **Prints, drawings, and photographs:** trace, reproducibility, negative/print relations, cropping, caption, and documentary claim.
5. **Modern and contemporary:** abstraction, construction, institutional framing, and refusal of resemblance.
6. **New Media:** time, software, interface, iteration, participation, and technological obsolescence.

## Object encounter card

Use this for five to eight objects. Depth is more useful than coverage.

### Object `[number]`

- **Gallery / accession:** `[fill]`
- **Before the label:** What do I perceive, at what scale and distance?
- **Material event:** What physically had to happen for this to exist?
- **Representational relation:** resemblance / trace / symbol / construction / performance / generation / other
- **Mediation:** tools, conventions, workshop, institution, restoration, display, reproduction
- **Authorship:** named maker, attributed maker, workshop, anonymous labor, commissioner, curator, audience
- **Time:** What past event, duration, body, or process remains present?
- **Belief:** What does the work or display encourage me to take as real, true, authentic, original, or valuable?
- **Freedom:** What is determined for me, and what remains ambiguous?
- **After the label:** What changed? Copy exact label language only if clearly marked as quotation.
- **Resistance:** How does this work complicate my thesis?
- **Sentence to preserve:** `[unpolished writer language]`

## Pause after every third object

- What pattern am I imposing?
- Which object refuses it?
- Am I confusing realism with resemblance?
- Am I treating the named artist as the whole causal history?
- Has the museum's classification changed what kind of thing I think the work is?
- Am I looking at the work, its institutional framing, or my own prior concept?

## Immediately afterward — 30 minutes without research

Write one page containing:

- the most resistant object;
- one sensory detail unavailable in an online reproduction;
- one moment where label or context altered perception;
- one authorship assumption that became unstable;
- one way the visit weakened or narrowed the thesis;
- one image or sentence that may carry the article's opening.

## The next morning — verification and criticism

1. Locate official museum records for any object likely to enter the article.
2. Verify artist, title, date, medium, provenance, gallery text, and accession number.
3. Separate three layers in your notes: `OBSERVATION`, `INSTITUTIONAL CLAIM`, and `INTERPRETATION`.
4. Select one object for sustained criticism. Describe formal relations before explaining them.
5. Ask how the object changes the theory; do not merely attach a Bazin quotation to it.

## AI interview protocol

Give AI only the article brief and raw field notes first. Ask it to interview you one question at a time in this order:

1. sensory and spatial detail;
2. sequence of attention;
3. what the label changed;
4. where interpretation exceeded observation;
5. what resisted the thesis;
6. which claim now needs external evidence.

Only after answering should you ask AI to group observations by theme. Preserve the interview separately so generated categories do not replace the original encounter.

## Done enough when

- Five to eight object cards exist, including one apparently irrelevant object.
- Raw observation is preserved separately from label information and interpretation.
- At least one object resists or narrows the thesis.
- Every museum fact proposed for publication has an official record.
- You have decided whether the walk belongs in the article, and why.

## AI prompt

“Interview me from these raw museum notes one question at a time. Begin with what I physically saw and when I noticed it. Challenge any historical or causal inference not supported by the notes. Do not outline the article until I have identified what resisted my thesis.”
