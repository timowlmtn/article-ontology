## Why this record exists

This peer-reviewed study tests a question central to synthetic-image provenance: whether people distinguish visual source from visual appearance, and whether their belief about source changes their aesthetic judgment.

## Study design

- **Sample:** 201 final participants from the UK, recruited through Prolific.
- **Stimuli:** 40 artworks shown in random order: 20 generated with Midjourney v4 and 20 human-made artworks selected from copyright-free online material.
- **Blinding:** Participants were not told the true origin of each image.
- **Per-image measures:** Four 0–100 sliders: liking; positive emotion; whether the image was most likely human-made (0) or AI-generated (100); and prior familiarity.
- **Additional measures:** Personality, empathy, creative identity, attitudes toward technology, and art interest/exposure questionnaires.

## Findings relevant to this project

- Participants did not reliably discriminate AI-generated from human-made images in this selected set.
- By actual source, the AI-generated images were liked more on average than the human-made images (59.2 versus 47.3, 0–100 scale).
- By *perceived* source, images judged more likely to be AI-generated were liked less and elicited less positive emotion, regardless of true source.

## Boundaries and cautions

- This is evidence about the study’s particular images and sample, not a universal finding that AI art is preferred or that people cannot identify all AI images.
- The researchers chose the human and AI stimuli themselves; they acknowledge the image set was not a direct, fair aesthetic comparison.
- Treat the study as evidence about attribution effects in evaluation, not as evidence of an image’s actual provenance or artistic value.
