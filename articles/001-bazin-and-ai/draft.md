What Would André Bazin Make of AI Slop?
From the earliest cave paintings, humans have created art to capture the experience of living. Art represents creations from our experience and imagination that capture our emotions, likenesses, fantasies, and thoughts. The media have always developed alongside technology: cave paintings, sarcophagi, daily life, plays, sacred texts, and the lives of the artists themselves.
Painting evolved as artists sought to make two-dimensional forms appear convincingly real. They developed perspective, anatomical study, and new approaches to light and shadow; some also used optical devices such as the camera obscura to aid observation and composition. Just as AI use can be difficult to detect today, an artist's use of technical tools is not always visible in the finished work.

October 29, 2013. https://www.getty.edu/news/getty-newly-acquired-rembrandt-now-on-view/.For example, "Rembrandt Laughing" is a self-portrait he captured with a masterful sense of a single moment in time. The image feels real in a way that we can relate to: through a smile, the moment right before one begins laughing, and the playful addition of a gorget to give the subject panache. He captures himself in a timeless moment. We don't know if he used a camera obscura to make such a realistic painting, but it has a photographic quality.
When he painted that self-portrait, many painters used the camera obscura to enhance their work, and it was widely considered a useful, practical tool. We don't know if Rembrandt used it in this case, but we do know he painted on copper, which made it easy to paint quickly and fix mistakes. Today, many authors use AI, and we often don't truly know how involved AI is in their work.
Andre Bazin is one of the film critics known for his philosophy of cinematic realism. His insights offer a structured way to analyze the relationship between the images captured by a camera and the composition and arrangement that turns those images into art. A painting can capture a mood and a moment, and a film can tell a story that speaks to our basic humanity. In his essay, "The Ontology of the Photographic Image," he posits that film and photography have a special relationship to reality because they mechanically record light as it is in a specific moment.

De Sica, Vittorio, director. *Ladri di biciclette* [*Bicycle Thieves*]. 1948. Italian with English subtitles. YouTube video, accessed September 6, 2026.The film "Bicycle Thieves" distills the relationship between reality and filming. De Sica used non-professional actors and set the film in post-war Rome in a way that captured both the hope and grinding desperation of poverty. The black-and-white setting and the simple, direct camera angles and scenes offer a clear view of the director's artistry.
Through the lens, we see every expression and moment clearly. Even though we have little in common with post-war Italy, we feel a timeless connection to the protagonist, Ricci, as he struggles to survive and support his family.
Hypothetically, what if we trained the AI to create a film masterpiece like Bicycle Thieves? We could likely copy the story, give it a modern setting, and generate scenes and characters based on a plot. But it would not be grounded in living acting, but rather a statistical space invented from the training - an imitation of a reflection, if you will.
Bazin talks about the power of film to capture a world that is at once a reality at a moment in time and an image-a hallucination that is also a fact. We believe in the stories films tell us because the director positions the cameras, the subjects, and the surroundings so that each snippet conveys a message, and the whole of the snippets conveys an entire story.
What if an AI can create the hallucination of a world that looks like reality and, within it, set a story of human struggle? Would the result be automatically AI slop? If the reason were that it lacked authenticity, because an AI inherently lacks authenticity, then that would ground our intuition that real subjects are defined by real people as a core requirement of artistic merit.
Even if the AI falls short, as has been my personal experience viewing AI-generated images, AI is now a part of the creative process in many cases. It is, along the trajectory from the camera obscura to the photograph to the film, a tool used by many creative artists, and it will be used more in the future.
As an experiment, I used the most Advanced ChatGPT 6.0 Astra to generate a black-and-white photograph of an Italian man circa 1948, just after he realizes his bicycle has been stolen.
OpenAI image generation system, prompted by the project writerThe result is remarkable at first glance: the details on the wall, the man's expression, his clothing- except until you notice he's holding a U-lock, which was patented in 1972 by Stan Kaplan. 
For someone recreating a film, such small details could be reworked. If you don't like the image, then you can regenerate it until you get something you like. However, a movie like Bicycle Thieves has around 128,000 frames, so creating a refined version would take significant effort. One might even posit that the creation of a masterpiece, driving AI to a desired artistic outcome, could be an auteur work of art in itself.
Compare the image to the actual scene of the movie, and it's easy to see the gap such an "AI filmmaker" would need to overcome. The authenticity of time and place is a truly immense gap. In the background, we see "Roma" written out. One of the posters is of Marlene Dietrich, a film star of the 1930s who had renounced her German citizenship in WWII and performed for the Allied troops. The actor himself was an out-of-work factory worker who understood firsthand the crushing nature of poverty. The genius of film lies in how it intersects history, reality, and artistry to create a masterpiece.
De Sica, Vittorio, director. *Ladri di biciclette* [*Bicycle Thieves*].The question remains: where does AI live in the genesis of technical aids to creation? Is it simply a continuation along the lines of the camera obscura to photographs to film, or are we on the cusp of a new artistic medium that incorporates AI into the creative process? Looking at the best of what AI has to offer vs. the best of what reality has to offer in the hands of a master filmmaker, I don't see AI as ever being qualified as an "auteur" in the way Bazin uses the term. It rather lives as the role of the camera or the film, or even the backdrops, lending aid where necessary, but missing that one essential quality of art: authenticity.
